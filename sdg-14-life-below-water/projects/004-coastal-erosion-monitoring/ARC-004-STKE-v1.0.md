## Executive Summary

### Purpose

This document identifies key stakeholders for the Coastal Erosion Monitoring platform, their underlying drivers, and how these manifest into goals. The platform will provide integrated coastal change mapping, erosion prediction, and risk communication for the approximately 6,000 km of English coastline at risk of erosion.

### Key Findings

Coastal erosion is a deeply personal issue for affected communities — homes, businesses, and infrastructure are physically lost to the sea. The strongest alignment is around providing better data to inform Shoreline Management Plan (SMP) policies and flood risk investment decisions. The most significant tension is between the scientific need for accurate, sometimes alarming erosion predictions and the political sensitivity of communicating that certain coastal communities will not be defended (the "managed realignment" or "no active intervention" SMP policies). A secondary tension exists between homeowners demanding protection and the EA's duty to allocate limited funding to the most cost-effective interventions.

### Critical Success Factors

- Integrate airborne LiDAR survey data, satellite imagery, wave buoy records, and ground survey data into a unified coastal change analysis platform
- Deliver credible erosion rate predictions that withstand scientific peer review and inform SMP policy decisions
- Provide public-facing coastal erosion information that is accurate, contextualised, and does not cause unnecessary alarm or false reassurance
- Enable local authority planners to access erosion predictions for planning application decisions
- Support EA business case development for coastal protection investment using HM Treasury Green Book methodology

### Stakeholder Alignment Score

**Overall Alignment**: LOW-MEDIUM

Deep disagreement between affected coastal communities (who want defence and protection) and EA/DEFRA policy (which accepts managed realignment in some locations as the most sustainable approach). Strong technical alignment between EA, BGS, and UKHO on data integration needs. Planning authorities need better erosion data but are concerned about the impact of publishing predictions on property values and community viability.

## Stakeholder Drivers Analysis

### SD-1: DEFRA Flood and Coastal Minister — Climate Adaptation and Community Resilience

**Stakeholder**: DEFRA Flood and Coastal Minister

**Driver Category**: POLITICAL / STRATEGIC

**Driver Statement**: Demonstrate effective government investment in coastal protection while honestly communicating the reality that not all coastline can be defended, building community resilience in areas where managed realignment is the policy.

**Context & Background**:
Climate change is accelerating coastal erosion through sea level rise, increased storm frequency, and changing wave patterns. The government's Flood and Coastal Erosion Risk Management (FCERM) Strategy 2020 acknowledges that some coastal change is inevitable and calls for a "resilience-based" approach. The Minister must balance the imperative to protect lives and property with the fiscal reality that defending every stretch of coastline is unaffordable and environmentally unsustainable. Communicating "no active intervention" policies to affected communities is one of the most politically sensitive aspects of the role.

**Driver Intensity**: CRITICAL

**Enablers**:

- Credible erosion prediction data that justifies investment decisions
- Clear visualisation of where coastal defences are and are not planned
- Evidence of coastal protection investment delivering measurable results
- Tools to support community engagement and adaptation planning

**Blockers**:

- Erosion predictions showing accelerating loss in politically sensitive areas
- Media coverage focusing on individual homeowner stories against "cold" data
- Parliamentary pressure for protection in economically unviable locations
- Uncertainty in climate projections undermining prediction credibility

**Related Stakeholders**: EA FCRM Head (investment), coastal communities (impact), HM Treasury (funding), CCC (climate adaptation)

### SD-2: EA NCMP Manager — National Survey Data Integration

**Stakeholder**: EA National Coastal Monitoring Programme Manager

**Driver Category**: OPERATIONAL

**Driver Statement**: Integrate the six regional coastal monitoring programmes into a single national platform with consistent data standards, automated processing, and accessible analytical tools, replacing the current patchwork of regional databases and delivery partners.

**Context & Background**:
The National Coastal Monitoring Programme surveys the English coastline using airborne LiDAR (every 1-2 years), aerial photography, beach profile surveys, and topographic surveys. The data is collected by six regional monitoring programmes (South-East, South-West, North-East, North-West, Anglian, and East Midlands) with different delivery partners, data formats, and quality standards. Integration is manual and time-consuming. The programme generates approximately 2TB of survey data annually, growing as survey frequency increases.

**Driver Intensity**: CRITICAL

**Enablers**:

- Standardised data format across all six regional programmes
- Automated LiDAR processing pipeline (point cloud to DEM to change analysis)
- Centralised data catalogue with version control and metadata
- Automated change detection between survey epochs

**Blockers**:

- Six different delivery partners with different technical capabilities
- Historical data in inconsistent formats requiring migration
- Survey scheduling constraints (weather-dependent, aircraft availability)
- Budget allocation across regional programmes creates coordination challenges

**Related Stakeholders**: Regional monitoring programmes, BGS (geological context), academic researchers (data users)

### SD-3: Coastal Local Authorities — Planning and Development Control

**Stakeholder**: Coastal local authority planning departments (approximately 100 authorities)

**Driver Category**: OPERATIONAL / COMPLIANCE

**Driver Statement**: Access reliable, current erosion prediction data to inform planning application decisions in coastal zones, fulfilling National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) requirements to consider coastal change.

**Context & Background**:
The NPPF requires local planning authorities to consider coastal change when determining planning applications. Many authorities have Coastal Change Management Areas (CCMAs) where development is restricted. However, the erosion data available to planners is often outdated (SMP data from 2010-2011 cycle), not at sufficient resolution for individual site decisions, and difficult to access. Planners need a system where they can enter a location and receive an erosion risk assessment with prediction timescales.

**Driver Intensity**: HIGH

**Enablers**:

- Location-based erosion risk lookup for planning applications
- Erosion prediction timescales at property-level resolution
- Downloadable evidence reports for planning decision documentation
- Integration with local authority planning portals

**Blockers**:

- Erosion predictions at insufficient spatial resolution for individual properties
- Uncertainty in predictions creating liability concerns for planning decisions
- Publishing erosion data that affects property values and planning blight
- Different local authorities with different planning policy approaches

**Related Stakeholders**: MHCLG (planning policy), homeowners (property values), insurance industry (risk assessment)

### SD-4: Coastal Communities — Protection, Compensation, and Truthful Communication

**Stakeholder**: Coastal communities and homeowners in erosion-affected areas

**Driver Category**: PERSONAL / FINANCIAL

**Driver Statement**: Receive honest, timely information about erosion risk to their properties, with clear communication about what protection is planned, what is not, and what support is available for relocation and adaptation.

**Context & Background**:
Approximately 700 properties in England are at risk of coastal erosion in the next 20 years, with several thousand more at risk within 50-100 years. Communities such as Happisburgh (Norfolk), Hemsby (Norfolk), Fairlight (East Sussex), and Skipsea (East Yorkshire) face the loss of homes and community infrastructure. Current erosion information is difficult to find, technically complex, and often delivered through insensitive bureaucratic processes. Homeowners want to know: "How many years do I have?" and "Will you defend my property?"

**Driver Intensity**: CRITICAL (for affected individuals)

**Enablers**:

- Plain-English erosion risk information accessible online for any coastal address
- Clear communication of SMP policy (hold the line/managed realignment/no active intervention)
- Early warning of accelerated erosion events (cliff collapses, beach drawdown)
- Information about adaptation support, compensation, and relocation assistance

**Blockers**:

- Erosion predictions presented as precise when they carry significant uncertainty
- Data published without community engagement causing panic or planning blight
- System designed for technical users without public accessibility
- Mismatch between community expectations (defence) and SMP policy (realignment)

**Related Stakeholders**: Local authorities (community liaison), DEFRA Minister (policy), National Trust (managed realignment estates), insurance industry

### SD-5: EA Head of FCRM — Investment Decision Evidence

**Stakeholder**: EA Head of Flood and Coastal Risk Management

**Driver Category**: FINANCIAL / STRATEGIC

**Driver Statement**: Use improved coastal erosion data to build stronger business cases for coastal protection investment, enabling HM Treasury approval for GBP 5.2 billion FCERM capital programme allocation.

**Context & Background**:
The government's six-year FCERM capital programme invests approximately GBP 5.2 billion in flood and coastal defences. Coastal protection projects compete for funding against flood risk reduction projects using the Partnership Funding calculator. Better erosion data — including projected rates, property counts at risk, and infrastructure impacts — enables more accurate benefit-cost ratios in business cases, potentially unlocking funding for projects that are currently borderline.

**Driver Intensity**: HIGH

**Enablers**:

- Accurate erosion rate projections linked to property and infrastructure databases
- Automated benefit-cost calculation for coastal protection options
- Climate change scenario modelling (UKCP18 sea level rise projections)
- Integration with the EA's FCERM programme management tools

**Blockers**:

- Erosion predictions with too wide uncertainty bands for investment appraisal
- HM Treasury Green Book requirements for monetised benefits that are hard to quantify for coastal erosion
- Competition for FCERM funding from flood risk projects with higher BCRs
- Political pressure to fund projects that do not meet economic appraisal thresholds

**Related Stakeholders**: HM Treasury (investment approval), coastal local authorities (partnership funding contributions), DEFRA Minister (programme prioritisation)

### SD-6: Network Rail — Infrastructure Risk Assessment

**Stakeholder**: Network Rail

**Driver Category**: OPERATIONAL / RISK

**Driver Statement**: Access coastal erosion prediction data for the 100+ km of railway line within 100m of eroding coastline, enabling proactive infrastructure resilience planning and avoiding reactive emergency closures.

**Context & Background**:
Several key rail routes run close to eroding coastlines — the Dawlish sea wall (South Devon main line), the Cumbrian Coast line, sections of the East Coast Main Line, and the Folkestone-Dover line. The Dawlish collapse in 2014 (which closed the main line for 8 weeks at a cost of GBP 1.2 billion to the regional economy) demonstrated the vulnerability. Network Rail needs erosion prediction data integrated into its asset management systems to plan resilience works before emergency closures become necessary.

**Driver Intensity**: HIGH

**Enablers**:

- API access to erosion prediction data for specific coastal sections
- Alert service for accelerated erosion near critical infrastructure
- Long-term (50-year, 100-year) erosion predictions for investment planning
- Integration with Network Rail's asset management system

**Blockers**:

- Erosion predictions not at sufficient spatial resolution for infrastructure sections
- Different timescales between rail investment cycles (5-year Control Periods) and erosion prediction horizons
- Institutional boundaries between EA coastal monitoring and Network Rail asset management
- Uncertainty in erosion predictions making rail investment decisions difficult

**Related Stakeholders**: National Highways (similar infrastructure concerns), EA FCRM Head (defence coordination), DEFRA Minister (strategic infrastructure)

## Conflict Analysis

### Conflict 1: Accurate Predictions vs Community Impact

**Stakeholders**: EA/Scientists (SD-2, SD-5) vs coastal communities (SD-4) vs local authorities (SD-3)

**Nature**: Accurate erosion predictions, once published, affect property values, insurance availability, and community viability. There is pressure to suppress or soften alarming predictions.

**Resolution Strategy**: PHASE — Engage communities before data publication. Provide predictions with clearly communicated uncertainty ranges. Couple erosion data with adaptation support information. Never suppress accurate data, but contextualise it sensitively.

### Conflict 2: Defence Investment vs Managed Realignment

**Stakeholders**: Coastal communities (SD-4) vs EA FCRM (SD-5) vs DEFRA Minister (SD-1)

**Nature**: Communities want their coastline defended. EA's economic appraisal often concludes managed realignment is the best value approach. The platform's data will inevitably support or undermine defence cases.

**Resolution Strategy**: INNOVATE — Platform presents multiple scenario options (defend, realign, no intervention) with costs and outcomes for each, enabling informed community dialogue rather than presenting a single predetermined conclusion.
