## Executive Summary

This document establishes the mandatory architecture principles governing all technology decisions across the SDG 7: Affordable and Clean Energy programme. These principles apply to five UK Government digital services:

- **001** — Smart Meter Data Platform (DESNZ)
- **002** — Renewable Energy Grid Management (National Grid ESO)
- **003** — Energy Performance Certificate System (DLUHC)
- **004** — Green Homes Grant Portal (DESNZ)
- **005** — Community Energy Fund Tracker (DESNZ)

**Scope**: All technology projects, systems, and initiatives within the SDG 7 programme
**Authority**: Enterprise Architecture Review Board
**Compliance**: Mandatory unless exception approved through the formal exception process (Section VI)

**Philosophy**: These principles are **technology-agnostic** — they describe WHAT qualities the architecture must have, not HOW to implement them with specific products. Technology selection happens during research and design phases guided by these principles.

**Governing Context**: These principles align with the GDS Service Standard, Technology Code of Practice (TCoP), NCSC Cyber Essentials Plus, UK GDPR, Data Protection Act 2018, Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018, the Energy Act 2023, the Net Zero Strategy, and the British Energy Security Strategy.

## I. Strategic Principles

### 1. User-Centred Design for Energy Consumers

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST be designed around the needs of end users — from householders viewing smart meter data to energy assessors submitting EPC ratings — with services that are simple, inclusive, and accessible to everyone who needs them.

**Rationale**:
The citizens and professionals served by these programmes span a wide spectrum: householders managing energy bills, vulnerable fuel-poor households, community energy volunteers, domestic energy assessors, and grid operators. The GDS Service Standard mandates user-centred design, and the Energy Act 2023 places new obligations on providing consumers with accessible energy data. Systems must not create barriers to participation in the clean energy transition.

**Implications**:

- Conduct user research with representative users, including fuel-poor households, elderly citizens, those with disabilities, and users with low digital literacy
- Design for assisted digital journeys — not all householders can self-serve online; telephony and face-to-face channels must be supported
- Meet accessibility standards (WCAG 2.2 Level AA minimum) as a legal requirement under the Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018
- Provide real-time energy data in formats understandable by non-technical consumers (kWh visualisations, cost projections, carbon impact)
- Support multiple languages where appropriate, particularly Welsh language requirements

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] User research conducted with representative sample including fuel-poor and digitally excluded households
- [ ] Accessibility audit passed at WCAG 2.2 Level AA
- [ ] Assisted digital pathway defined and tested for all citizen-facing services
- [ ] Content reviewed for plain language and energy terminology glossary provided
- [ ] User satisfaction metrics defined and baseline established
- [ ] Service assessed against GDS Service Standard points 1–3

### 2. Scalability for National Energy Data Volumes

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST be designed to scale horizontally to handle the data volumes generated by 53 million smart meters, national EPC assessments, and real-time grid telemetry, with the ability to dynamically adjust capacity based on load patterns.

**Rationale**:
The UK smart meter rollout targets 53 million meters transmitting half-hourly consumption data — approximately 2.5 billion readings per day. Grid management requires sub-second telemetry from thousands of renewable generation assets. EPC assessments generate seasonal spikes around property transaction peaks. Systems must handle both sustained high-volume data ingestion and acute demand spikes without degradation.

**Implications**:

- Design for stateless data ingestion components that can be replicated to handle peak meter reading volumes
- Avoid hard-coded limits on data volumes; design for at least 10x growth beyond current meter population
- Use event-driven architectures to decouple data producers (meters, sensors, assessors) from data consumers (analytics, billing, policy)
- Implement auto-scaling based on ingestion rate metrics and queue depths
- Partition data by geographic region, meter type, or time window to enable parallel processing

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] System can ingest 2.5 billion+ readings per day with linear scaling
- [ ] No single points of failure that limit data throughput
- [ ] Load testing demonstrates capacity growth with added resources at national scale
- [ ] Scaling metrics and triggers defined with documented thresholds
- [ ] Cost model accounts for variable capacity and seasonal energy demand patterns

### 3. Resilience for Critical Energy Infrastructure

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST gracefully degrade when dependencies fail and recover automatically without data loss, recognising that energy data platforms are part of the UK's critical national infrastructure.

**Rationale**:
Smart meter data feeds billing systems that affect household finances. Grid management platforms support national electricity balancing — an outage could contribute to grid instability. The DESNZ and National Grid ESO operate within the NIS Regulations (Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018) framework for operators of essential services. System failures have direct consequences for energy security and consumer welfare.

**Implications**:

- Implement circuit breakers for all external dependencies, including DCC (Data Communications Company) interfaces and National Grid APIs
- Use timeouts on all network calls to DCC, energy supplier systems, and Ofgem registers
- Retry with exponential backoff and jitter for transient failures in meter data collection
- Graceful degradation: if real-time data is unavailable, serve cached data with clear staleness indicators
- Automated health checks and self-healing for data ingestion pipelines
- Bulkhead isolation between smart meter data processing, grid telemetry, and consumer-facing services
- Comply with NIS Regulations incident reporting requirements for essential services

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Failure modes identified and mitigated for all critical energy data paths
- [ ] Fault injection testing performed on DCC interface and grid telemetry pipelines
- [ ] Recovery Time Objective (RTO) and Recovery Point Objective (RPO) defined per service
- [ ] Automated failover tested and documented
- [ ] Degraded mode behaviour documented with consumer-facing messaging defined
- [ ] NIS Regulations compliance assessment completed

### 4. Interoperability with the Energy Ecosystem

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST expose functionality through well-defined, versioned interfaces using industry-standard protocols and energy-sector data standards. Direct database access across system boundaries is prohibited.

**Rationale**:
The SDG 7 programme must integrate with a complex ecosystem: the Data Communications Company (DCC) for smart meter communications, SMETS2 meter protocols, Ofgem's Central Switching Service, HMRC for green finance tax relief, local authority planning systems, National Grid ESO's balancing mechanisms, and the Microgeneration Certification Scheme (MCS). The Technology Code of Practice mandates open standards, and the energy sector has specific data interchange formats that must be supported.

**Implications**:

- Use DCC-standard DLMS/COSEM protocols for smart meter data interchange
- Implement MPAN (Meter Point Administration Number) as the canonical identifier for meter points
- Publish RESTful APIs conforming to GDS API standards for cross-government integration
- Support CIM (Common Information Model IEC 61968/61970) for grid management data exchange
- Version all interfaces with backward compatibility guarantees and published deprecation schedules
- No direct database access across system boundaries; all cross-system data flows via published APIs or events

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Interface specifications published using OpenAPI 3.0 or AsyncAPI for event-driven interfaces
- [ ] DCC integration tested against DCC's User Integration Testing (UIT) environment
- [ ] MPAN-based addressing validated for all meter data flows
- [ ] Versioning strategy defined with deprecation policy
- [ ] Authentication and authorisation model documented for all integration points
- [ ] Compliance with GDS API technical and data standards verified

### 5. Security by Design (NON-NEGOTIABLE)

**Principle Statement**:
All architectures MUST implement defence-in-depth security with zero-trust principles. Security is NOT a feature to be added later — it is a foundational requirement. Energy data systems are high-value targets for state-sponsored and criminal threat actors.

**Rationale**:
Smart meter data reveals household occupancy patterns, lifestyle habits, and vulnerability indicators. Grid management systems, if compromised, could affect national energy security. The NCSC has identified energy infrastructure as a priority target for hostile state actors. UK GDPR, the Data Protection Act 2018, and the NIS Regulations impose strict security obligations. The Smart Metering Equipment Technical Specifications (SMETS) mandate specific security controls for meter communications.

**Zero Trust Pillars**:

1. **Identity-Based Access**: No network-based trust; every request authenticated and authorised, including machine-to-machine DCC communications
2. **Least Privilege**: Grant minimum necessary permissions; time-boxed access for maintenance operations on grid systems
3. **Encryption Everywhere**: Data encrypted in transit and at rest; smart meter data encrypted using DCC-mandated PKI infrastructure
4. **Continuous Verification**: Monitor, log, and analyse all access patterns; anomaly detection for unusual meter data patterns that may indicate tampering

**Mandatory Controls**:

- [ ] Multi-factor authentication for all staff and administrative access to energy data systems
- [ ] Service-to-service authentication using mutual TLS or signed tokens for DCC and grid integrations
- [ ] Secrets managed via a dedicated secrets management solution (never in code, config, or environment variables)
- [ ] Network segmentation with minimal trust zones; operational technology (OT) networks isolated from IT networks
- [ ] Encryption at rest for all data stores containing personal energy consumption data
- [ ] Encrypted transport for all network communication including DCC WAN interfaces
- [ ] Structured, immutable audit logging of all access to energy consumption data
- [ ] Regular security testing including penetration testing, vulnerability scanning, and DCC security assessments

**Compliance Frameworks**:

- NCSC Cyber Essentials Plus
- NCSC Secure by Design
- UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018
- NIS Regulations 2018 (for operators of essential services)
- HMG Security Policy Framework
- SMETS2 security requirements (DCC)
- OWASP Top 10

**Exceptions**:

- NONE. Security principles are non-negotiable.
- Specific control implementations may vary with documented compensating controls.

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Threat model completed and reviewed for each service, including energy-sector-specific threats
- [ ] Security controls mapped to requirements and compliance obligations including NIS Regulations
- [ ] Security testing plan defined and executed before go-live
- [ ] Incident response runbook created with defined escalation paths including NCSC notification
- [ ] DCC security assessment completed and approved for smart meter data access

### 6. Observability and Operational Excellence

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST emit structured telemetry (logs, metrics, traces) enabling real-time monitoring of energy data flows, grid telemetry, and consumer service health.

**Rationale**:
Energy data platforms operate at national scale with strict timeliness requirements. A failure to ingest smart meter data may go undetected for hours without proper observability, resulting in billing errors and consumer complaints. Grid management requires sub-second visibility into system health. Observability is a first-class architectural requirement.

**Telemetry Requirements**:

- **Logging**: Structured logs with correlation IDs linking meter readings through the full ingestion pipeline
- **Metrics**: Data ingestion rates per DCC region, meter read success/failure ratios, API latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99), grid telemetry freshness
- **Tracing**: Distributed trace context propagated across DCC interface, data processing, and consumer API layers
- **Alerting**: SLO-based alerting with actionable runbooks; specific alerts for meter data ingestion lag and grid telemetry staleness

**Required Instrumentation**:

- Meter data ingestion volume, success rate, and latency per DCC communication service provider
- EPC assessment submission volume and processing time
- Grant application processing time and conversion rates
- Grid telemetry freshness and coverage by renewable generation type
- Security events (authentication failures, unusual data access patterns, potential meter tampering indicators)

**Log Retention**:

- **Security/audit logs**: Minimum 2 years (aligned with UK Government retention standards and NIS Regulations)
- **Application logs**: 90 days minimum for troubleshooting
- **Energy consumption data logs**: 13 months minimum (Ofgem requirement for billing disputes)
- **Metrics**: 2 years with progressive aggregation for trend analysis and policy evaluation

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Logging, metrics, and tracing instrumented for all services
- [ ] Dashboards configured for operational and business metrics including meter data health
- [ ] Service Level Objectives (SLOs) and Service Level Indicators (SLIs) defined
- [ ] Runbooks created for all alerting scenarios including DCC communication failures
- [ ] Capacity planning metrics tracked and reviewed monthly

## II. Data Principles

### 7. Energy Data Sovereignty and Governance

**Principle Statement**:
Data classification, residency, retention, and access controls MUST comply with UK GDPR, the Data Protection Act 2018, the Smart Energy Code (SEC), and departmental data governance policies. Energy consumption data is personal data and MUST be treated accordingly.

**Rationale**:
Smart meter data — half-hourly consumption readings — is personal data under UK GDPR as it can reveal household occupancy, lifestyle patterns, and vulnerability. The Smart Energy Code governs access to and use of smart meter data through the DCC. Ofgem's data access and privacy framework imposes additional obligations. Mishandling energy data causes direct consumer harm and regulatory penalties.

**Data Classification Tiers**:

1. **OFFICIAL**: Aggregated, anonymised energy statistics; published EPC ratings; public grant programme data
2. **OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE**: Individual smart meter readings, household energy consumption profiles, vulnerability indicators, grant application details, individual EPC assessment data

**Data Residency**:

- All personal energy data MUST reside within UK sovereign data centres
- No transfer of personal energy data outside the UK without a lawful basis and DPIA
- Cross-departmental data sharing (DESNZ, DLUHC, Ofgem, HMRC) MUST be governed by data sharing agreements compliant with the Digital Economy Act 2017 and the Smart Energy Code

**Data Retention**:

- Smart meter consumption data: Retained in accordance with SEC obligations and Ofgem licence conditions
- EPC data: 10-year retention aligned with EPC validity period
- Grant application data: 7 years post-completion for audit purposes (HM Treasury requirements)
- Automatic deletion or anonymisation after defined retention periods expire
- Legal hold process documented for regulatory investigations or Ofgem enforcement

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Data classification performed for all energy data stores and data flows
- [ ] UK residency confirmed for all personal energy data storage and processing
- [ ] Retention policies configured with automated deletion or anonymisation
- [ ] Data sharing agreements in place for all cross-departmental energy data flows
- [ ] Data Protection Impact Assessment completed for all services handling personal energy data

---

### 8. Energy Data Privacy by Design

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST embed privacy protections into the architecture from the outset, minimising personal energy data collection, processing, and retention to what is strictly necessary for the stated purpose.

**Rationale**:
UK GDPR Article 25 mandates data protection by design and by default. Half-hourly smart meter data can reveal intimate details about household life — when residents wake, eat, sleep, and whether they are at home. The Smart Energy Code requires explicit consumer consent for access to granular consumption data. Privacy is not merely a compliance obligation but a consumer trust requirement that underpins public acceptance of the smart meter programme.

**Implications**:

- Collect only the minimum granularity of energy data required: use daily aggregates where half-hourly data is not necessary
- Implement purpose limitation — consumption data collected for billing MUST NOT be repurposed for commercial profiling without explicit consent
- Pseudonymise or anonymise energy data wherever possible, especially for policy analysis and aggregate reporting
- Provide consumers with clear, accessible privacy notices explaining what energy data is collected, who can access it, and why
- Support data subject rights: access to personal energy data, rectification, erasure, portability (including Data Portability under Smart Energy Code)
- Implement differential privacy or k-anonymity techniques for aggregate energy statistics published for policy evaluation
- Honour consumer consent preferences for data sharing through the DCC consent framework

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) completed for each service handling personal energy data
- [ ] Data minimisation review conducted — no unnecessary granularity of energy data collected
- [ ] Consumer consent mechanisms implemented for granular data access (aligned with SEC)
- [ ] Privacy notices published in plain language explaining energy data use
- [ ] Data subject rights mechanisms implemented and tested
- [ ] Pseudonymisation or anonymisation applied to analytics and reporting data

### 9. Energy Data Quality and Lineage

**Principle Statement**:
Data pipelines MUST maintain data quality standards and provide end-to-end lineage for auditability, recognising that energy data directly informs billing, policy decisions, carbon accounting, and consumer financial outcomes.

**Rationale**:
Inaccurate smart meter data leads to incorrect bills and consumer financial harm. Poor EPC data leads to incorrect energy efficiency ratings and misallocated grants. Grid telemetry errors could contribute to balancing failures. Ofgem requires energy suppliers to demonstrate data accuracy in meter reading processes. Lineage enables accountability when billing disputes or policy evaluations are challenged.

**Quality Standards**:

- **Completeness**: No gaps in half-hourly consumption data without documented reasons; missing reads flagged within 24 hours
- **Consistency**: Cross-system reconciliation between DCC data, supplier billing data, and settlement data
- **Accuracy**: Meter read validation against expected consumption profiles; outlier detection for reads outside 3 standard deviations
- **Timeliness**: Half-hourly data available within 4 hours of collection; daily aggregates by 06:00 next day; EPC data within 24 hours of assessment

**Lineage Requirements**:

- Source-to-target mapping documented for all energy data flows from DCC through processing to consumer presentation
- Transformation logic (unit conversions, tariff calculations, carbon intensity mappings) version-controlled and auditable
- Data quality metrics tracked per pipeline with alerting on degradation
- Impact analysis capability for schema changes across the DCC, settlement, and billing data chain

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Data quality rules defined and automated with monitoring for all energy data pipelines
- [ ] Lineage metadata captured and queryable from meter to consumer-facing output
- [ ] Data contracts defined between DCC, energy suppliers, and government analytics platforms
- [ ] Schema evolution strategy documented with backward compatibility approach

### 10. Single Source of Truth for Energy Data Domains

**Principle Statement**:
Every energy data domain MUST have a single authoritative source. Derived copies MUST be clearly labelled and synchronised with defined freshness guarantees.

**Rationale**:
The energy sector has well-established authoritative sources: the DCC for smart meter readings, the EPC Register for building energy ratings, the Central FIT Register for feed-in tariff installations, and Ofgem's licensing register for suppliers. Duplicating these without clear provenance creates inconsistency, reconciliation overhead, and incorrect policy decisions.

**Implications**:

- DCC is the system of record for smart meter communications and readings
- The EPC Register (managed by the Landmark/Quidos consortium) is the system of record for Energy Performance Certificates
- Ofgem registers are authoritative for supplier licensing, FIT/SEG registrations, and Renewable Obligation Certificates
- MPAN database (managed by Electralink) is authoritative for meter point identification
- Derived copies in programme systems are read-only caches with documented freshness and synchronisation frequency
- Avoid bidirectional synchronisation — always flow from authoritative source to derived copy

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] System of record identified and documented for each energy data entity
- [ ] Derived copies documented with synchronisation frequency and acceptable staleness
- [ ] No bidirectional sync without a documented conflict resolution strategy
- [ ] Master data management approach defined for shared reference data (geographic regions, tariff codes, fuel types)

## III. Integration Principles

### 11. Loose Coupling Across the Energy Data Landscape

**Principle Statement**:
Systems MUST be loosely coupled through published interfaces, avoiding shared databases, shared file systems, or tight runtime dependencies between smart meter, grid, EPC, grant, and community energy services.

**Rationale**:
The SDG 7 programme spans five projects across three departments (DESNZ, DLUHC, National Grid ESO) plus arm's-length bodies (Ofgem, DCC). Each team must develop, deploy, and evolve their service independently. The DCC infrastructure imposes specific integration patterns that must not leak into internal system coupling.

**Implications**:

- Communicate through published APIs or asynchronous events — never through shared databases
- Each system manages its own data lifecycle and data store; the smart meter platform does not directly query the EPC database
- DCC integration isolated behind an adapter layer so that DCC protocol changes do not cascade through the system
- Shared libraries kept minimal; favour duplication over coupling where the cost of coordination exceeds the cost of duplication
- Avoid distributed transactions across system boundaries; use compensating actions or sagas for cross-service workflows (e.g., grant application linked to EPC data)

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] All inter-system communication uses APIs or events, not direct data store access
- [ ] No shared mutable state across system boundaries
- [ ] Each system has its own independent data store
- [ ] Deployment of one system does not require simultaneous deployment of another
- [ ] DCC integration isolated behind an adapter with documented interface contract

---

### 12. Event-Driven Integration for Energy Data Flows

**Principle Statement**:
Systems SHOULD use asynchronous, event-driven communication for non-real-time interactions to improve resilience and decoupling across the energy data ecosystem.

**Rationale**:
Smart meter data flows are inherently asynchronous — meters transmit readings that are collected and processed in batches or near-real-time streams. Grid telemetry, while requiring lower latency, follows a publish-subscribe pattern. EPC assessments trigger downstream workflows (grant eligibility, council tax adjustments) that do not require synchronous responses. Event-driven patterns reduce temporal coupling and improve fault tolerance across departmental boundaries.

**When to Use Asynchronous Patterns**:

- Smart meter data ingestion from DCC (batch and near-real-time readings)
- Cross-departmental notifications (new EPC triggers grant eligibility check, meter installation triggers data access setup)
- Non-real-time business processes (grant application processing, community fund allocation)
- Aggregate analytics and policy reporting pipelines
- Audit trail events and compliance logging

**When Synchronous Communication is Acceptable**:

- Real-time consumer queries (current meter reading, live grid carbon intensity)
- EPC lookup during property transaction (immediate response needed)
- Grant eligibility check during application submission
- Authentication and authorisation decisions

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Asynchronous patterns used for meter data ingestion and non-real-time cross-system flows
- [ ] Message durability and delivery guarantees defined (at-least-once for meter data, exactly-once for financial transactions)
- [ ] Event schemas versioned and published in a shared schema registry
- [ ] Dead letter handling and error recovery procedures defined for failed meter data processing
- [ ] Event replay capability available for meter data reprocessing and dispute resolution

## IV. Quality Attributes

### 13. Performance at National Energy Scale

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST meet defined performance targets under expected and peak load, optimised for the unique characteristics of energy data processing at national scale.

**Rationale**:
Householders accessing their smart meter data expect sub-second responses. Grid operators require sub-second telemetry for balancing decisions. The EPC system experiences peak loads during property transaction surges (typically spring and early autumn). Grant portals experience extreme spikes when new schemes are announced. Performance must be defined and tested for these energy-sector-specific load patterns.

**Performance Targets** (to be defined per service):

- **Smart Meter Consumer Portal**: p95 page load < 2 seconds; API response < 200ms
- **Grid Management Dashboard**: Telemetry refresh < 1 second; balancing calculation < 500ms
- **EPC Submission**: Assessment submission acknowledged < 3 seconds; certificate generated < 30 seconds
- **Grant Portal**: Application submission < 5 seconds; eligibility check < 2 seconds
- **Data Ingestion**: 2.5 billion meter reads/day sustained throughput; peak burst capacity 5x baseline

**Implications**:

- Performance requirements defined before implementation, informed by DCC data volumes and user research
- Load testing performed with realistic meter data volumes (53M meters, half-hourly reads)
- Performance monitoring continuous with alerting on degradation
- Caching strategies for frequently-accessed energy data (current tariff rates, carbon intensity, EPC lookups)
- Optimise consumer portal for low-specification devices on slow connections (fuel-poor households disproportionately affected)

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Performance requirements defined with measurable targets per service
- [ ] Load testing performed at national scale data volumes
- [ ] Performance metrics monitored in production with alerting
- [ ] Capacity planning model defined accounting for smart meter rollout trajectory

### 14. Availability for Essential Energy Services

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST meet defined availability targets with automated recovery and minimal data loss, reflecting the essential nature of energy services to UK households and grid stability.

**Rationale**:
Smart meter data platforms feed billing systems — unavailability means estimated bills and consumer complaints. Grid management systems support national electricity balancing — downtime could contribute to supply/demand imbalance. The NIS Regulations impose specific availability obligations on operators of essential services. Availability targets must reflect service criticality and the impact of downtime on consumers and grid operations.

**Availability Targets**:

- **Grid Management Platform**: 99.99% uptime (52.6 minutes downtime per year); RTO < 5 minutes
- **Smart Meter Data Platform**: 99.95% uptime (4.4 hours per year); RTO < 15 minutes; RPO < 1 hour
- **EPC System**: 99.9% uptime (8.7 hours per year); RTO < 1 hour
- **Grant Portal**: 99.9% uptime; RTO < 1 hour (critical during application windows)
- **Community Energy Tracker**: 99.5% uptime; RTO < 4 hours

**High Availability Patterns**:

- Redundancy across multiple UK availability zones or data centres
- Automated health checks with self-healing for data ingestion pipelines
- Active-active configuration for grid management; active-passive for other services
- Regular disaster recovery testing (quarterly for grid management, annually for others)
- Planned maintenance windows outside peak energy usage periods (avoid 16:00–20:00 winter evenings)

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Availability SLA defined per service based on citizen and grid impact assessment
- [ ] RTO and RPO requirements documented and achievable with current architecture
- [ ] Redundancy strategy implemented and tested
- [ ] Failover tested regularly with documented results
- [ ] Backup and restore procedures validated through regular testing

### 15. Maintainability for Evolving Energy Policy

**Principle Statement**:
All systems MUST be designed for change, with clear separation of concerns, modular architecture, and sufficient documentation, recognising that energy policy evolves rapidly in response to Net Zero commitments.

**Rationale**:
Energy policy changes frequently: tariff structures evolve, carbon intensity calculation methods are updated, EPC rating methodologies are revised, new grant schemes are announced and existing ones closed, and the regulatory framework adapts to emerging technologies (hydrogen, battery storage, vehicle-to-grid). The Energy Act 2023 alone introduced numerous new regulatory instruments. Systems must accommodate policy changes without fundamental re-architecture.

**Implications**:

- Externalise business rules (tariff calculations, EPC scoring algorithms, grant eligibility criteria, carbon intensity factors) so policy changes require configuration not code deployments
- Modular architecture separating energy-domain logic from infrastructure concerns
- Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) for all significant technical choices
- Automated testing sufficient to validate policy rule changes across the full range of scenarios
- Version energy calculation methodologies (EPC SAP methodology, carbon factors) to enable retrospective analysis

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Architecture documentation exists and is current
- [ ] Business rules externalised and configurable without code deployment
- [ ] Automated test coverage enables safe policy rule updates
- [ ] Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) document key choices
- [ ] Energy calculation methodology versions tracked and auditable

### 16. Accessibility and Digital Inclusion

**Principle Statement**:
All citizen-facing systems MUST meet WCAG 2.2 Level AA as a minimum and MUST be designed to be usable by the widest possible range of users, with particular attention to fuel-poor and digitally excluded households.

**Rationale**:
The Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018 make WCAG 2.2 Level AA a legal requirement. Fuel-poor households — the primary beneficiaries of the Green Homes Grant and energy efficiency programmes — disproportionately include elderly citizens, people with disabilities, and those with limited digital access. The smart meter rollout specifically targets universal coverage including prepayment meter users, who are statistically more likely to be in fuel poverty. Inaccessible systems exclude the very households that clean energy programmes are designed to help.

**Implications**:

- Design using progressive enhancement — core functionality works without client-side scripting
- Test with assistive technologies (screen readers, voice control, switch access)
- Support standard text resizing and high-contrast modes
- Ensure energy data visualisations have text alternatives and are not colour-dependent
- Provide clear explanations of energy terminology and units (kWh, standing charges, tariff rates)
- Support offline capabilities where possible for areas with poor connectivity (rural areas with high renewable energy potential)
- Welsh language support for all citizen-facing services

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] WCAG 2.2 Level AA compliance verified through automated and manual testing
- [ ] Tested with at least two assistive technologies
- [ ] Keyboard-only navigation tested for all user journeys
- [ ] Accessibility statement published and maintained
- [ ] Service usable on low-specification devices and slow connections
- [ ] Welsh language version available for citizen-facing services

## V. Energy-Specific Principles

### 17. Net Zero Alignment

**Principle Statement**:
All technology decisions MUST consider their contribution to the UK's Net Zero target. Systems SHOULD enable carbon reduction measurement and reporting, and their own operational carbon footprint MUST be minimised.

**Rationale**:
The UK has a legally binding commitment to achieve Net Zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050, with an interim target of a 68% reduction by 2030. Every system in this programme exists to support that transition. The technology itself must not undermine the goal — data centres and cloud computing consume significant energy. The Greening Government ICT Strategy requires departments to measure and reduce the environmental impact of their technology estates.

**Implications**:

- Select cloud regions and data centres powered by renewable energy where available
- Optimise data processing to minimise computational waste (efficient algorithms, right-sized infrastructure)
- Enable carbon impact measurement in all energy-related services (carbon intensity of electricity consumed, carbon saved by EPC improvements, carbon reduction from community energy projects)
- Use GHG Protocol Scope 1/2/3 methodology for carbon accounting within digital services
- Report technology carbon footprint as part of departmental sustainability reporting
- Design data retention policies to avoid storing unnecessary data (storage has a carbon cost)

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Cloud provider renewable energy commitment documented
- [ ] Carbon impact measurement capability built into energy-facing services
- [ ] Technology carbon footprint estimated and included in sustainability reporting
- [ ] Data retention policies consider environmental impact of unnecessary storage
- [ ] Green Software Foundation principles applied to architecture decisions

### 18. Smart Meter Data Privacy and Consumer Rights

**Principle Statement**:
Smart meter data access MUST comply with the Smart Energy Code (SEC), Ofgem's data access and privacy framework, and UK GDPR. Consumer consent MUST be obtained, recorded, and honoured for all granular energy data access.

**Rationale**:
The Smart Energy Code establishes a detailed framework for who can access smart meter data, at what granularity, and for what purposes. Half-hourly consumption data requires explicit consumer consent. The DCC's consent framework provides the technical mechanism. Ofgem's data access and privacy framework sets the policy context. Failure to comply risks regulatory enforcement, consumer backlash, and undermining public trust in the smart meter programme.

**Implications**:

- Implement the DCC consent framework for all granular data access
- Distinguish between data access levels: monthly aggregates (no consent needed for regulated purposes), daily aggregates (consent required for third-party access), half-hourly data (explicit consent required)
- Record all consent decisions with timestamp, scope, and revocation capability
- Implement "privacy dashboard" functionality allowing consumers to view and manage their data sharing preferences

### 19. Infrastructure as Code

**Principle Statement**:
All infrastructure MUST be defined as code, version-controlled, and deployed through automated pipelines. Manual changes to production infrastructure are prohibited.

**Rationale**:
Energy data platforms operate at national scale with strict security and availability requirements. Manual infrastructure changes create configuration drift, inconsistency, and undocumented state. Infrastructure as Code enables repeatability, auditability, and disaster recovery — critical for NIS-regulated essential services.

**Implications**:

- All infrastructure defined in declarative code within version control
- Infrastructure changes go through the same code review process as application code
- Environments are reproducible from code — no snowflake configurations
- No manual changes to production infrastructure (enforced through access controls)
- Infrastructure code versioned and deployed alongside application code

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] All infrastructure defined as code in version control
- [ ] Infrastructure code goes through peer review before deployment
- [ ] Environments reproducible from code (tested through regular rebuilds)
- [ ] No manual infrastructure changes in production (enforced, not just policy)

### 20. Automated Testing for Energy System Correctness

**Principle Statement**:
All code changes MUST be validated through automated testing before deployment, with specific attention to energy calculation correctness, tariff accuracy, and regulatory compliance.

**Rationale**:
Errors in energy calculations — tariff application, EPC scoring, carbon intensity factors, grant eligibility — have direct financial and policy consequences. An incorrect tariff calculation applied to millions of meters creates mass billing errors. An incorrect EPC algorithm produces invalid ratings that affect property values and grant eligibility. Automated testing must cover energy-domain-specific scenarios comprehensively.

**Implications**:

- Unit tests for all energy calculations (tariff application, EPC SAP methodology, carbon intensity, grant eligibility rules)
- Integration tests for DCC data ingestion and processing pipelines
- End-to-end tests for critical consumer journeys (view consumption, apply for grant, submit EPC)
- Regression tests for policy rule changes (new tariff structures, updated carbon factors, revised EPC methodology)
- Property-based testing for energy calculations (verify mathematical properties hold across input ranges)

**Validation Gates**:

- [ ] Automated tests exist and pass before code is merged
- [ ] Energy calculation test suites cover all known regulatory scenarios
- [ ] Test coverage meets defined thresholds per service
- [ ] Critical user journeys have end-to-end tests
- [ ] Performance and security tests run regularly in the pipeline
